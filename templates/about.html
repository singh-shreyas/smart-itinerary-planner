About & methodology

How the planner thinks

An Analysis and Design of Algorithms project. It splits a hard, real planning problem into textbook sub-problems, solves each with a classic algorithm, and is honest about where the guarantees — and the data — stop.

The problem

A traveller has a few days and far more attractions than time. Each attraction has a value (rating, popularity and whether it matches the traveller's interests), a visit duration, an entry fee and opening hours that differ by weekday. Moving between places takes time, and lunch and dinner have to fit in too. We must choose which attractions to visit on which day, and in what order, so the plan is worth as much as possible without breaking the daily time window, any opening hours or the meal windows. This is a travel-optimisation system: it optimises time and travel, not money.

This is the multi-day Orienteering Problem with Time Windows, which is NP-hard. So we do not claim optimal itineraries. We decompose it into sub-problems that each have a well-understood algorithm: shortest paths, selection, route ordering and scheduling.

The pipeline

Dijkstra runs once per city (or group of nearby cities). Then each day goes through filter → select → route → repair → fill, in date order.

Scoring and the knapsack model

Where is travel time in the knapsack? Travel depends on the order, which is decided after selection. So each stop's weight is visit + pace buffer + cheapest trip out of it, and the capacity is the day length minus the shortest first ride, the unavoidable extra of the ride home (beyond the last stop's cheapest exit) and the meal breaks. Every route leaves each stop exactly once, so these weights can only under-estimate the real day: the knapsack never throws away a set that truly fits. The scheduler then checks the real timeline, and repair fixes optimism.

Discretisation. Weights are rounded up to {{ cfg.DP_TIME_UNIT_MIN }}-minute units and the capacity down. Every strategy receives the identical integer instance.

What "optimal" means here. DP (and Max visits) are exact for this rounded knapsack model on each day. 2-opt reaches a local optimum. Held–Karp is exact for travel only, ignores opening hours, and is used on the comparison page as a lower bound. None of this makes the whole multi-day itinerary provably optimal.

They use the same candidates, weights, costs, capacities, routing, meals, repair and validation, so differences come only from the objective. Max visits gives every place the value B + score with B = Σ scores + 1: one extra place always outweighs any difference in score, so the DP maximises the count first and the value second.

Three itinerary modes

What to see and how to spread it are two separate questions. The places are either the ones you pick, or the ones the selection strategy above chose for your days. The mode then assigns them to days, and each mode ranks the feasible assignments by its own objective. All three use the same feasibility check: a day only counts if the planner's router finds an order that the minute-by-minute scheduler accepts, with opening hours, meals, treks, ferries and the ride back included.

The search (algorithms/day_partition.py) is construct-and-improve: hardest places first into the cheapest feasible day, plus a first-fit-decreasing start (the classic bin-packing rule), then local search that moves one place to another day or swaps two. Every distinct day is routed once and remembered. A comfortable day is out and about for at most {{ (100 * cfg.COMFORT_SHARE)|round|int }}% of your daily window and never more than {{ cfg.COMFORT_MAX_MIN // 60 }} hours, so one more day is used only if it removes at least 30 minutes of over-long days. The tests check the minimum number of days against exhaustive search on small cases.

Access: treks, ferries and walks

Not every sight is at the end of a road. A trek, a ferry crossing or a walk is an edge of the same graph as the roads, so Dijkstra finds mixed routes, such as the road to Gaurikund followed by the trek, and every leg lists its parts with their own times. The trek up and the trek down are separate directed edges, so each is counted once: in the knapsack weight, in routing, in the timeline and in the day's totals. The traffic factor applies to road parts only. A ferry with a known first and last departure makes you wait for the first one, and rules the trip out after the last one.

Times come only from sources: a duration a source states (Kedarnath trek “6 to 8 hours”: we use 8), or Naismith's rule applied to a sourced distance and climb (5 km/h plus 1 hour per 600 m ascent), labelled as an estimate. Helicopters, ropeways and battery cars are listed as other ways in, but never used in a plan, because their schedules and availability are not verified. A place whose access time is unknown is never given a guessed time. It is listed with its reason and a note to verify locally.

Where you stay

Every city offers five stay areas: North, South, East, West and Central. Each one is a real neighbourhood, suburb or town from OpenStreetMap, never a sight, a railway station or an airport, and preferably one with many places to stay nearby (counted from OpenStreetMap's hotels and guest houses). The direction is the compass sector seen from the city centre. Where the geography makes a direction meaningless, for example where the sea or a mountain valley lies that way, the closest area by direction is used and marked with * and a note that says where it really is. Stations remain in the data as transport points, kept apart from places to stay.

The stay area is a node of the travel graph with its own road links (OSRM), so every first ride, ride home, route order and travel score is computed from it. Changing the area changes the travel times; the cached tables are keyed to the database file, so they are rebuilt when the data changes. A stay area is a neighbourhood's map point, not a specific hotel.

Using the whole day

A longer day raises the knapsack's capacity, so more places can be selected; the fill step then inserts any leftover candidate that still fits. When hours stay unused the day summary says why — places closing before you could reach them, meals, or simply nothing left that matches your settings — instead of quietly leaving the evening empty. Each day shows sightseeing, local and intercity travel, meals, waiting, pace buffer and unused time separately.

Lunch and dinner

Meals are their own timeline entries with a window and a length (defaults: lunch {{ cfg.DEFAULT_LUNCH[0] }}–{{ cfg.DEFAULT_LUNCH[1] }} for {{ cfg.DEFAULT_LUNCH[2] }} min, dinner {{ cfg.DEFAULT_DINNER[0] }}–{{ cfg.DEFAULT_DINNER[1] }} for {{ cfg.DEFAULT_DINNER[2] }} min). A meal is planned only if it cannot be had entirely before your day starts or after it ends, so a short day never gets an unnecessary dinner. The scheduler takes a meal as soon as its window is open after a visit, or earlier if the next visit would make it too late. If a listed food place is within {{ cfg.MEAL_VENUE_MAX_DETOUR_MIN }} planned minutes, the meal goes there (the detour is a separate travel leg); otherwise it is a generic break where you are, and no venue is claimed.

Food and shopping

Food streets, markets and malls are attractions with food or shopping categories. They are optional: planned as stops only when you choose a matching food or shopping interest. Food places also serve as meal venues, filtered by your preferred style and by “vegetarian only”, which uses only places a source explicitly describes as vegetarian. Shopping stops can take your chosen time.

Every listed place comes from a Wikipedia article (name, location, description); there are no invented restaurants, ratings or addresses. Coverage is uneven, so the planner says when an interest has little data in your cities. More listings could later be imported from OpenStreetMap (ODbL, free, fair-use limits) — see scripts/discover_food_shopping.py.

Nearby-city trips

Add up to {{ cfg.MAX_EXTRA_CITIES }} cities to your base city. Rule: every pair of cities must be within {{ cfg.MULTI_CITY_MAX_KM }} km of each other in a straight line between their centres (not just each within {{ cfg.MULTI_CITY_MAX_KM }} km of the base). Straight-line distance is only a proxy, so road distance is shown where we have it. The graph joins the cities with OSRM road links; where none exists the planner uses a clearly labelled estimate (km × {{ cfg.ESTIMATE_DETOUR_FACTOR }} at {{ cfg.ESTIMATE_SPEED_KMH }} km/h), never zero. You stay at your base and travel out each day; each day is planned around one city or several, whichever gives the best result, so the plan does not zig-zag between cities.

How the comparison page scores strategies

Raw metrics come from the actual plans. Each is normalised against the best strategy on the same trip (100 = best of the three), then weighted: {% for k, w in cfg.SCORECARD_WEIGHTS.items() %}{{ (100 * w)|round|int }}% {{ k }}{{ ', ' if not loop.last else '.' }}{% endfor %} There is no cost component: this is a travel-optimisation system, and an earlier “budget” option, which the planner could not honestly optimise, has been removed. Its 10% went half to travel efficiency and half to value. Feasibility is a gate — a plan with any constraint violation is never recommended — and runtime is reported but not scored. Ties are reported as ties, and identical timelines as identical.

Reproducible plans

No step uses randomness or the iteration order of a set; every choice breaks ties by value, then travel, then the place's row in the dataset. Each itinerary shows four fingerprints: inputs, data (a hash of the CSV files, currently #{{ (meta.dataset_fingerprint or '')[:10] }}), settings (planner constants in config.py) and the resulting plan. Same inputs, data and settings always give the same plan. The benchmark script uses fixed random seeds.

Locations

Wikipedia article coordinates, cross-checked against Wikidata and OpenStreetMap; disagreements over 2 km were reviewed by hand. Places with no reliable location are left out and listed on the Destinations page. {{ access_rows|length }} places reached on foot, by boat or by ferry are kept, with their access modelled as described under Access.

Travel times

Directed road links from OSRM on OpenStreetMap roads (each place to its 5 nearest neighbours, plus intercity links), fetched once offline. Free-flow times × an assumed traffic factor of {{ TRAFFIC_FACTOR }}, rounded up to {{ cfg.TRAVEL_ROUNDING_MIN }} minutes for planning. No live traffic.

Popularity and ratings

Popularity for the newer cities = log-scaled Wikipedia page views over 12 months (fixed bounds: 1,000 → 0, 3,000,000 → 1). No open source of visitor ratings exists, so those places are “not rated” and scored with a neutral {{ cfg.RATING_PRIOR }} prior. The four original cities keep their hand-chosen, illustrative ratings.

Hours, fees, visit times

From OpenStreetMap when tagged in a format we can read; otherwise the documented default for that kind of place (below), marked estimate everywhere. Fees are for Indian adults and shown for information only; the planner does not optimise cost.

Limitations

  • The full problem is NP-hard; select-then-route is a decomposition heuristic and can miss a better combination (DP can lose to Greedy on a whole trip).
  • When the planner chooses the places, it does so day by day in date order (myopic); the itinerary mode then re-assigns that set to days, but does not swap in places the selection left out.
  • Assigning places to days is NP-hard; the modes use a heuristic search, checked against exhaustive search only on small cases.
  • Most hours, fees and visit times in the newer cities are category defaults; closing days are unknown.
  • One base for the whole trip: overnight moves between cities are not modelled. Multi-city eligibility uses straight-line distance.
  • Trek and walking times are estimates from cited distances and climbs; ferry timetables and weather closures are not live. Places with unknown access times are not scheduled.
  • You return to the same stay area every night: overnight stops on a trek (for example at Kedarnath) are not modelled.
  • Stay areas are OpenStreetMap neighbourhood points; the “places to stay nearby” count depends on OSM coverage, which is thin in small towns.
  • Food and shopping coverage depends on Wikipedia and is thin in small towns.
  • Map lines are straight segments in visit order, not road geometry.
